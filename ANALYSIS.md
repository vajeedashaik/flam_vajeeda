# Deep Analysis — `flam_vajeeda` ("The Main Submission")

This project was read at the same depth as the four `flam demo` variations analyzed previously (`adaptive-layout-engine`, `adaptive-layout-engine-1`, `Adaptive-layout-engine-2`, `FlamaAi`), and is compared against them directly throughout using the detailed findings from those four ANALYSIS.md files.

## What this is

A candidate-generation-and-scoring engine — architecturally the closest of all five projects to `adaptive-layout-engine-1` ("the Maximalist") and `FlamaAi` — with two ingredients neither of those two, nor either of the other two projects, actually has: an explicit **Experience Graph** that derives relational edges between elements from role semantics (not just per-element constraints), and a **Context Engine** that collapses a surface's real-world constraints into six named qualitative flags which demonstrably change *which strategy wins*, not just how big elements are drawn. The project was built in six disciplined, specification-driven phases (`phase1.md`–`phase6.md`), each with its own acceptance criteria, and both README.md and ARCHITECTURE.md map every feature explicitly to a weighted grading rubric (constraint resolution 35%, layout correctness 25%, TypeScript/architecture 20%, example app 10%, code quality 10%) — the most rubric-literate of the five submissions.

## Architecture

```
AdSpec ─▶ buildGraph ─▶ ExperienceGraph (nodes + proximity/exclusion edges)
                                │
SurfaceProfile ─▶ resolveContext ─▶ Context (6 qualitative flags)
                                │
                    generateCandidates (4 strategies, ALL context-biased)
                                │
                    scoreCandidate (6 weighted sub-scores + hard-fail→0)
                                │
                    resolveLayout picks highest overall → DecisionTrace
```

Every strategy (`vertical-stack`, `horizontal-split`, `grid`, `overlay-safe-margins`) runs through exactly one shared placement primitive, `placeElementsInOrder()` in `candidates.ts` — verified by reading the full file: the priority-monotone shrink-then-drop cascade, the min-size floor check, the in-bounds check, and the overlap check are written once and reused by all four strategies. This is the same discipline `adaptive-layout-engine`'s `stack.ts` has and `Adaptive-layout-engine-2` and `FlamaAi` both lack (both of those independently reimplement placement per strategy, with `Adaptive-layout-engine-2` duplicating it across 4 functions and `FlamaAi` across 5, each ~90-230 lines of copy-adapted logic). Of all five projects in this set, this one and `adaptive-layout-engine` are the only two with a single, genuinely shared placement primitive.

## Where it went right

**1. The Experience Graph is a real architectural idea none of the four sibling projects attempt.** `graph.ts`'s `buildGraph()` derives two edge types from role semantics alone — a `proximity` edge between every `secondary` and every `action` element ("price/disclaimer is only meaningful beside its CTA"), and an `exclusion` edge between every `hero` and every `branding` element ("the hero and the brand mark must never overlap"). None of `adaptive-layout-engine`, `adaptive-layout-engine-1`, `Adaptive-layout-engine-2`, or `FlamaAi` model *relationships between elements* at all — every one of them scores/places elements independently, one at a time, using only each element's own role/priority/size. This project's graph is the only relational model in the set, and the rule set is genuinely id-agnostic (keyed on `role`, never on specific ids), matching the same "generalizes to any spec" discipline the four siblings' best work shows for surfaces.

**2. The Context Engine explicitly changes *which layout wins*, not just element sizes — and this is verified, not just claimed.** `contextFitScore()` in `scoring.ts` rewards the strategy whose geometry matches the surface's aspect-ratio class, rewards fewer visible elements under a short attention budget or far viewing, and penalizes `overlay-safe-margins` under touch (spread corners are harder to reach with a thumb). ARCHITECTURE.md documents a concrete before/after: adding `contextFit` flipped `broadcastLowerThird`'s winning strategy from `grid` to `horizontal-split` — a wide, far-viewing surface now genuinely prefers the strategy whose own geometry runs along the wide axis. I traced this directly in `scoring.ts:326-354` and it holds up: the bonus/penalty table is real code, not aspirational documentation. `adaptive-layout-engine-1`'s rubric has no equivalent "does the surface's real-world context change the winning strategy" mechanism — its context-like signals (touch, far-viewing) mostly bias *sizing* the way this project's `toRequest()` also does; `FlamaAi`'s strategy-affinity bonuses in `scoring.ts` are the closest sibling analogue (e.g. `compactHorizontalStrip` getting +350 for a wide canvas), but those are keyed only on raw aspect ratio, not on a Context Engine synthesizing touch/attention/viewing-distance into the decision the way this project's does.

**3. Triple-redundant, genuinely independent invariant enforcement.** The no-overlap / in-bounds guarantee is checked three separate times by three separately-written pieces of code: inside `placeElementsInOrder` (per-element, during placement), inside `scoring.ts`'s `anyOverlap`/`anyOutOfSurface` (per-candidate, during scoring — these hard-fail a candidate to 0), and inside `stress-lab.ts`'s `findInvariantViolation()` (end-to-end, on the actual chosen output, written "independently of scoring.ts so it is a genuine cross-check, not the scorer asserting about itself" — a comment I verified is true: `findInvariantViolation` re-implements the bounds and overlap checks from scratch rather than importing them). This is a more independently-implemented defense-in-depth than `FlamaAi`'s comparable design (which reuses one `validateResolvedLayout` function three times — elegant, but a single function's own bug would go uncaught by all three call sites, whereas here a bug in one of the three independent implementations would very likely be caught by the other two disagreeing).

**4. Genuinely honest, well-calibrated self-critique of its own headline metric.** The Stress Lab's "~65% robustness" number is explicitly and correctly flagged, in both README and ARCHITECTURE, as a *quality* bar (score ≥ 70) rather than a *correctness* bar — and the project separately reports that hard-invariant safety (`failed = 0`) held on 100% of 200 randomized surfaces every run. Distinguishing "the layout is valid" from "the layout is good" and refusing to let a flattering aggregate number imply more than it does is a more mature piece of self-assessment than any of the four sibling projects' own limitation sections attempt — `adaptive-layout-engine-1` comes closest by naming its rubric weights as "hand-picked, not calibrated," but doesn't have an analogous headline-metric-could-mislead callout.

**5. Real text measurement with an honestly-scoped fallback, exercised by a real adversarial scenario.** `text-measure.ts` uses actual Canvas `measureText()` in the browser and a documented linear approximation (`length × fontSize × 0.52`) in the Vitest node environment — the same category of honest disclosure as `FlamaAi`'s 0.55-ratio heuristic and `Adaptive-layout-engine-2`'s "no text measurement" admission, except this project's fallback is demonstrably exercised: the self-healing scenario's German CTA (~40% longer than the English) is measured and re-resolved rather than overflowing, and this is asserted directly in `resolver.test.ts`'s self-healing test, not just narrated in a demo.

**6. `explainLoss()` is a sharp, minimal piece of explainability.** Given any losing candidate's score breakdown and the winner's, it finds the single sub-score with the widest gap and names it with the actual point delta ("Scored 12 points lower on context fit (88 vs 76) — its strategy or element count didn't suit this surface's aspect ratio…"). This is deterministic and derived entirely from the two `ScoreBreakdown` objects — verified in `trace.ts` — no hardcoded per-candidate text. It's a tighter, more surgical version of `adaptive-layout-engine-1`'s `narrate.ts` (which synthesizes a fuller 3-5 sentence narrative); this project's version is less prose-rich but is arguably a cleaner mechanism for the specific "why did the runner-up lose" question a Layout Counterfactuals panel needs to answer.

**7. The unknown-surface path is a real proof, not a staged demo.** `UnknownSurfaceInput.tsx` calls the *exact* `defineSurface()` factory every built-in surface uses, and on a validation error shows the real thrown message verbatim (I traced this end-to-end: `defineSurface`'s cross-field check in `surfaces.ts:160-165` produces the exact string the component renders unmodified). This matches the spirit of `adaptive-layout-engine`'s and `FlamaAi`'s "no hardcoded surface IDs" source-scan tests, but goes one step further by also live-wiring a real user-facing failure path, which none of the four siblings' unknown-surface-style features demonstrate quite this directly.

## Where it went wrong

**1. The Experience Graph's edges are computed, tested, and never consumed — and this is honestly disclosed, but it means the project's single most distinctive idea is currently inert.** I confirmed by searching every file in `src/` that `graph.edges` and `getRelatedNodes()` are referenced only inside `graph.ts` itself and `graph.test.ts` — no strategy in `candidates.ts`, no rule in `scoring.ts`, and no component reads edge data at all. The README and ARCHITECTURE §10 do disclose this ("no strategy reads edge data for placement yet"), which keeps the project's honesty record intact — but it means the "secondary belongs beside its action" proximity rule has no corresponding downstream check anywhere (nothing in `visualBalanceScore` or elsewhere measures element-to-element adjacency), and the "hero excludes branding" rule's guarantee is entirely subsumed by the blanket "no two visible elements may ever overlap" rule that already applies to every pair, not just hero/branding. Practically: the Experience Graph currently does no more work than a plain list of nodes would. This is the single highest-leverage next feature to build, precisely because the hard part (deriving correct, id-agnostic relational edges) is already done — turning it into an actual `adjacencyFit` sub-score (reward proximity-edge pairs that end up spatially close; the exclusion rule needs no separate enforcement since overlap is already forbidden globally) is a comparatively small addition with an outsized payoff, since it would make this project's most unique architectural idea actually influence output.

**2. Three declared surface fields are validated and populated but never read downstream — and, unlike the Experience Graph gap, this is *not* disclosed anywhere.** I verified by grep that `minTextSize` is validated in `surfaces.ts`, populated on all five sample surfaces and the self-healing scenario, but never read by `context.ts`, `candidates.ts`, or `scoring.ts` — there is no `textLegibilityCompliance`-style sub-score analogous to the `tapTargetCompliance` sub-score that `minTapTarget` *does* drive (that field is used for both context-aware CTA sizing and a real scoring penalty). `hasAudio` and `allowsMotion` are likewise derived into `Context` and covered by a context test, but nothing in `candidates.ts` or `scoring.ts` ever reads either flag. None of the three appears in the Known Limitations list in README or ARCHITECTURE §10, which otherwise has an excellent disclosure record (it does disclose the analogous `alignment`-edges gap) — this is the one place the project's self-assessment isn't quite as complete as it is everywhere else. `minTextSize` in particular is the easiest of the three to fix, since `minTapTarget`'s exact pattern (context-aware inflation + a compliance sub-score with a violation penalty) already exists in the codebase as a template to copy.

**3. The Stress Lab's independent invariant check doesn't cover all of scoring's hard-fail conditions.** `findInvariantViolation()` in `stress-lab.ts` re-checks bounds and overlap independently of `scoring.ts` — a genuine, valuable cross-check for those two conditions. But `scoreCandidate`'s hard-fail rule also fires on `dropsAnAlwaysElement` and `hasPriorityInversion`, and `findInvariantViolation` does not independently re-derive either of those from the resolved output — it only sees the visible-element geometry. In practice this doesn't currently mislabel anything (a candidate that hard-fails only on a dropped-always-element still scores 0, which is `< 70`, so it lands in "degraded" rather than silently "passed") — but it does mean "degraded" is currently overloaded to mean two different things (merely tight-but-valid layouts, and layouts that actually violated the "always visible" guarantee), which the Stress Lab's UI and reason strings don't currently distinguish. A surface where every candidate drops the headline would report as "just" degraded rather than surfacing the more serious "an always-visible element was dropped everywhere" condition distinctly.

**4. `resolveLayout` has no explicit validity flag for callers.** Even when every candidate hard-fails (overall = 0), `resolveLayout` still returns a `layout` and a `winningStrategy` — there's no `{ valid: boolean, violations: [...] }` on the result the way `FlamaAi`'s `ResolvedLayout.validation` gives a caller a direct, structured answer to "is this actually usable, and if not, why." A consumer of this engine (as opposed to the demo app, which has the full `DecisionTrace` to inspect) would have to manually re-derive "all four candidates scored 0" from `trace.candidateScores` rather than checking one boolean. This is a real, if minor, API-ergonomics gap relative to `FlamaAi`'s more explicit validity contract.

**5. Same category of "un-tuned constants" limitation as the two most sophisticated siblings — self-disclosed, not a differentiator.** The `contextFit` bonus/penalty magnitudes (`+20`/`−15` shape bonuses, `1.3×`/`1.15×` scale factors) and the six sub-score weights are hand-chosen and documented as such, exactly the same honest limitation `adaptive-layout-engine-1`'s rubric constants and `FlamaAi`'s scoring-formula constants carry. Worth naming for completeness, but it isn't something this project does worse than its most rigorous siblings — all three are in the same position, and all three name it themselves.

**6. Narrower engineering breadth than the single most ambitious sibling.** Relative to `adaptive-layout-engine-1` specifically: no CLI, no property-based fuzzing (the Stress Lab's `mulberry32`-seeded random surfaces are a good, reproducible stress mechanism but not a shrinking property-tester), no visual regression suite, and only one renderer (`render-dom.ts` is a deliberate, honestly-labeled stub, same as `FlamaAi`'s single-renderer scope). This mirrors exactly the same gap `adaptive-layout-engine` and `Adaptive-layout-engine-2` have against the same sibling — this project is not behind its whole peer group here, only behind the one project that specifically optimized for engineering breadth.

**7. Not yet deployed.** README states "Not yet deployed" under Live demo, while `adaptive-layout-engine-1` ships a live Vercel deployment. Given this project's own README explicitly rehearses "resolve an unseen surface live in the interview" as a feature, a zero-friction live link is a small, high-value gap to close before an actual interview.

This project is the only one of the five with a genuine relational model between elements, and the only one that demonstrates — with a concrete before/after strategy-flip, not just an aspirational claim — that surface context changes which spatial strategy wins rather than just resizing elements within a fixed choice. It matches `adaptive-layout-engine`'s discipline of one shared placement primitive (where `Adaptive-layout-engine-2` and `FlamaAi` both duplicate placement logic per strategy), and its invariant-checking is the most genuinely independent of any project in the set. Its remaining gap against the whole group is engineering breadth — CLI, multiple renderers, true property-based fuzzing, visual regression, deployment — all of which belong almost exclusively to `adaptive-layout-engine-1`, and none of which are architecturally hard to add given how cleanly this project's core is already separated into `graph` / `context` / `candidates` / `scoring` / `resolver` / `trace`.

## Concrete, prioritized improvements

1. **Wire the Experience Graph's edges into scoring.** Add an `adjacencyFit`-style sub-score (or fold it into `visualBalance`) that rewards candidates where every `proximity`-linked pair (e.g. `price` beside `cta`) ends up spatially close — Euclidean centroid distance normalized by box size is a direct, cheap metric, and `getRelatedNodes(graph, id, "proximity")` already gives you the pairs to check. This is the single highest-leverage change available: it activates the project's most distinctive, currently-inert idea, and the hard part (correct, id-agnostic edge derivation) is already built and tested.

2. **Give `minTextSize` a consumer, mirroring `minTapTarget`'s existing pattern exactly.** Add a far-viewing-style inflation for text elements against `surface.minTextSize` in `toRequest()`, and a `textLegibilityCompliance` (or folded-into-`constraintViolations`) penalty in `scoring.ts` when a visible text element's rendered font size implies a smaller-than-`minTextSize` result. Then either wire `hasAudio`/`allowsMotion` into a real decision too (e.g., a candidate that assumes motion-based reveal order when `allowsMotion` is false) or add all three fields to the Known Limitations list alongside the already-disclosed `alignment`-edges gap — closing the one honesty gap this project currently has relative to its otherwise excellent disclosure record.

3. **Borrow `adaptive-layout-engine`'s enforced-typecheck-fixture pattern for `spec.invalid-example.ts.txt`.** Right now that file is documentation only (excluded from the build, not wired into any test). Converting it to a `@ts-expect-error`-based fixture checked by `tsc --noEmit` the way `adaptive-layout-engine`'s `spec.typecheck.ts` does would turn "these are compile errors" from a claim a reader has to trust into an enforced regression that fails the build the moment a change accidentally makes one of those examples legal.

4. **Extend `findInvariantViolation` to independently re-derive all of scoring's hard-fail conditions, not just bounds/overlap.** Add checks for "a `visibility: always` element was dropped" and "priority inversion" directly against the resolved output (both are simple, and the logic already exists once in `scoring.ts` — the point is a second, independent implementation, the way bounds/overlap already have one). This also lets the Stress Lab distinguish "degraded because merely tight" from "degraded because an always-visible guarantee was actually violated" — currently the same bucket.

5. **Add a `valid`/`violations` field to `ResolveResult`**, similar in spirit to `FlamaAi`'s `ResolvedLayout.validation` — a direct boolean plus reasons, computed once inside `resolveLayout` from the same hard-fail logic already being run, so a caller (or a future non-demo integration) doesn't have to manually scan `trace.candidateScores` to learn the layout may not be usable.

6. **Ship a live deployment.** This is the cheapest, highest-visibility gap: `adaptive-layout-engine-1` already proves a Vite+React app in this shape deploys cleanly to Vercel, and this project's own README explicitly rehearses "resolve an unseen surface live in the interview" as a selling point — a dead link where that demo should be is the one place presentation undersells substance that is otherwise there.

7. **Lower priority, for later:** consider `fast-check`-style property-based testing (with shrinking) as a complement to the Stress Lab's seeded-random sweep, and a second renderer (even a minimal Canvas or SVG path reading the same `ResolvedLayout`) to actually demonstrate the "renderer-agnostic by construction" claim the way the current stub can't. Both are exactly the two things separating this project from matching `adaptive-layout-engine-1`'s breadth, but both are lower-leverage than items 1-2 above given this project's specific strengths.

## Test coverage verdict

74 `it`/`test` blocks across 11 Vitest suites (the README's "86 tests" figure likely counts individual assertions generated by in-source loops, such as `resolver.test.ts`'s per-surface loop over the five sample surfaces, as separate reported tests) covering: every core module in isolation (`graph`, `context`, `spec`, `surfaces`, `candidates`, `scoring`, `trace`, `text-measure`), a dedicated determinism check on `scoreCandidate`, a source-level regex check that `candidates.ts`/`scoring.ts`/`resolver.ts` contain no hardcoded sample-surface ids (the same technique `FlamaAi` uses, independently arrived at), a 200-surface stress sweep with a documented pass/degrade/fail tiering, and a self-healing integration test asserting the CTA survives every simultaneous failure mode at once. This is comparable in breadth to `adaptive-layout-engine`'s suite and a clear step ahead of `Adaptive-layout-engine-2`'s (zero tests), though it does not reach `adaptive-layout-engine-1`'s property-based fuzzing or visual regression, and it lacks `FlamaAi`'s single most adversarial test (an engineered-to-be-unsatisfiable surface asserting graceful `valid: false` rather than merely a low score) — recommendation 5 above would make that exact test straightforward to add here too.

## Overall take

Of the five projects now analyzed, this is the one whose central architectural idea — that a layout resolver should reason about *relationships* between elements and about a surface's *real-world context*, not just each element's own constraints — is the most genuinely novel, and the Context Engine's effect on strategy choice is the one claim in this whole set that I could verify with a concrete, named before/after (the `broadcastLowerThird` strategy flip). It matches the most disciplined sibling's placement architecture (one shared primitive, not four or five duplicated ones) and has the most genuinely independent invariant checking of the group. Its two real gaps are, first, that its most distinctive feature (the Experience Graph) is currently decorative rather than load-bearing — a well-disclosed but real "not yet wired up" — and second, that it trails the single most engineering-ambitious sibling (`adaptive-layout-engine-1`) on breadth: no CLI, one renderer, no true property-based testing, no deployment. Neither gap requires new ideas to close, only wiring up ideas the project has already correctly designed — which is a materially better position to be in than either of the two projects with a genuine, undisclosed correctness bug or a completely absent test suite.
